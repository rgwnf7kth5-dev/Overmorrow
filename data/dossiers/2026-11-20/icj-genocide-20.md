# icj-genocide-20
- **Claim:** In its judgment of Thursday 19 November 2026 in The Gambia v. Myanmar, the International Court of Justice finds that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, i.e. breached its obligation under the Genocide Convention not to commit genocide.
- **Criterion:** YES if the operative paragraph (dispositif) of the merits judgment in case 178 (Application of the Genocide Convention, The Gambia v. Myanmar: 11 States intervening), delivered on 19 Nov 2026 (Europe/Amsterdam), contains a finding that Myanmar has violated (breached) its obligation not to commit genocide under Article I read with Article III(a) of the Genocide Convention (or words to the same effect, e.g. that Myanmar "has committed genocide" or is responsible for acts of genocide against the Rohingya). Evidence: the judgment or the ICJ press release on icj-cij.org, or reports by Reuters, AP, AFP or BBC quoting the operative paragraph. NO if the dispositif finds only breaches of the duties to prevent or punish genocide, of conspiracy, incitement, attempt or complicity, or of the provisional-measures order, or finds no breach; NO also if no judgment is delivered on 19 Nov 2026.
- **Event window:** 2026-11-19T00:00+01:00 – 2026-11-19T23:59+01:00 (Europe/Amsterdam); reading scheduled 10:00.
- **Evidence expected:** ICJ press release summarising the judgment and the judgment text on icj-cij.org, usually within hours of the reading; evidence_at 2026-11-19T14:00+01:00.
- **Deadline (resolves by):** 2026-11-21T05:00+01:00

## Facts
- Gambia's submissions: it asked the Court to declare that Myanmar breached the Convention "including by committing genocide, conspiring to commit genocide, failing to take any steps to prevent genocide, and failing to bring to trial people suspected of genocide", and to find that Myanmar failed to comply with the January 2020 provisional measures order — https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/11/03/questions-and-answers-international-court-of-justice-judgment-in-myanmar-genocide (published 2026-11-03T15:00-05:00, read 2026-11-17T17:53Z) "Gambia asked the ICJ to declare that Myanmar breached several obligations under the Genocide Convention, including by committing genocide".
- Gambia argues intent under the existing test: "the only inference that can reasonably be drawn from the facts" is intent to destroy; it relies on the Croatia v. Serbia (2015) test and on indicators (brutality, hate rhetoric, failure to investigate), and focuses on the villages Min Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung Nu — HRW Q&A (same URL) "In doing so, it applies a test set out by the court in 2015".
- Myanmar's case: the 2016–17 operations were counter-terrorism operations against ARSA; there is "no direct evidence of genocidal intent"; it contests the weight of the UN Fact-Finding Mission reports, "which it asserts to be the centerpiece of Gambia's case" — HRW Q&A (same URL).
- Myanmar at the January hearing: allegations "unsubstantiated"; "Emotional language and blurry factual pictures are not a substitute for a rigorous presentation of facts" (Ko Ko Hlaing) — AFP via https://www.justiceinfo.net/en/168072-top-un-court-to-rule-nov-19-on-myanmar-rohingya-genocide-case.html (published 2026-11-03T14:53Z, read 2026-11-17T17:53Z).
- Evidence record: Gambia relied on UN Fact-Finding Mission reports, the IIMM, satellite imagery and NGO findings; Rohingya witnesses were examined in closed sittings — https://www.globalr2p.org/publications/the-concluding-stage-of-the-gambia-v-myanmar-genocide-case-before-the-international-court-of-justice/ (published 2026-02-10T18:01Z, read 2026-11-17T17:53Z) "The Gambia relied on the reports of the United Nations Fact-Finding Mission, the International Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar, satellite imagery".
- Global Centre R2P: the case "could become the first time that the ICJ holds a state liable under state responsibility for the commission of genocide, instead of only for a failure to prevent and punish it" — same URL.
- Earlier stages: provisional measures ordered unanimously in January 2020; preliminary objections rejected in July 2022 and jurisdiction affirmed "by fifteen votes to one" — globalr2p (same URL). Myanmar has filed 12 compliance reports; Gambia says they give "no reason to conclude" compliance — HRW Q&A.
- Precedent Bosnia v. Serbia (2007): genocide at Srebrenica found but not attributed to Serbia; Serbia breached duties to prevent and punish; standard: "fully conclusive" evidence, intent inferred from a pattern must be "the only reasonable inference" — https://euobserver.com/248744/the-impact-of-the-icjs-genocide-ruling-in-myanmar-will-go-far-beyond-asia/ (Christine Van den Wyngaert, published 2026-11-13T11:21+01:00, read 2026-11-17T17:52Z) "it did not find that the acts of genocide could be attributed to Serbia".
- Precedent Croatia v. Serbia (2015): both claim and counterclaim rejected; "neither side had established the specific intent" — euobserver (same URL).
- Composition: ad hoc judges Navi Pillay (chosen by Gambia) and Claus Kreß (chosen by Myanmar) — euobserver (same URL).
- Bench questions in January: Judge Hmoud asked whether the dolus specialis for State responsibility differs from individual criminal responsibility; Judge Cleveland asked about the weight of NGO reports; Judge Gómez Robledo asked about adverse inferences from refusal to admit investigators — https://opiniojuris.org/2026/09/14/hard-facts-and-the-threshold-what-the-gambia-v-myanmar-should-not-decide/ (published 2026-09-14T12:00Z, read 2026-11-17T17:53Z) "Judge Hmoud asked whether individual criminal responsibility and State responsibility for genocide constitute distinct regimes".
- Standard-of-proof debate: intervening states press for a "comprehensive and holistic" assessment; US UN ambassador Mike Waltz warned the Court not to "lower the legal threshold for proving genocide"; FDD writes "There is strong evidence that Myanmar's actions have met the ICJ's current high standard of proof" and that both the Biden and Trump administrations characterised the Rohingya as victims of genocide — https://www.fdd.org/analysis/will-the-international-court-of-justice-lower-the-bar-for-genocide/ (published 2026-11-05T17:14Z, read 2026-11-17T17:52Z).
- Interveners (Art. 63, construction of the Convention only, no part in the merits hearings): Belgium, Canada, Denmark, DR Congo, France, Germany, Ireland, Maldives, Netherlands, Slovenia, UK — euobserver; HRW Q&A.
- Just Security (Jan 2026): the legal burden stays with Gambia, but if Myanmar offers no alternative explanation "it is highly likely to lose" — https://www.justsecurity.org/129532/proving-genocide-burden-proof/ (published 2026-01-23T13:55Z, read 2026-11-17T17:55Z).
- The judgment is final and without appeal — HRW Q&A.
- Context: the military that ran the 2017 operations seized power in 2021; Min Aung Hlaing became president after the 2025–26 elections — HRW Q&A. The ICC prosecutor sought a warrant for Min Aung Hlaing in Nov 2024 (crimes against humanity of deportation and persecution); Argentina issued warrants in Feb 2025 — HRW Q&A.

## Base rate
- Class: ICJ merits judgments on claims that a respondent State committed genocide under the Genocide Convention. Bosnia v. Serbia 2007: commission not found. Croatia v. Serbia 2015: commission not found (both claim and counterclaim). Rate of commission findings 0/2 (n=2; source euobserver above). No entry in pipeline/reference-data applies.
- Difference from both precedents (sources above): here the acts were carried out directly by the State's own armed forces (no attribution problem like the Bosnian Serb forces in 2007), and the respondent's defence rests on an alternative (counter-insurgency) intent.

## Risks
- Wording: the Court may find breach of Article III(a) for specific episodes only (e.g. certain villages or the August–September 2017 operations). Any such finding in the dispositif counts YES.
- The Court may find acts of genocide (actus reus) established but not the specific intent, as in Croatia v. Serbia — NO.
- The Court may find breach of the duty to prevent/punish only, as in Bosnia v. Serbia — NO.
- A finding of conspiracy or incitement but not commission — NO under this criterion.
- Press summaries may say "genocide" loosely; resolution must rest on the dispositif text.
